Historical and Philosophical Psychology

How have European philosophers understood emotions, desire and love? This course follows changing accounts of the soul, moral judgement and human flourishing from classical philosophy to psychoanalysis.

Gao Yuan / European Philosophy of Emotions
01 / COURSE OVERVIEW

Emotions in the history of European thought

Emotions are central to philosophical questions about the self, virtue, judgement and the good life. This course examines how European thinkers have explained their origins, assessed their moral significance and explored possibilities for their transformation.

The course begins with Plato and Aristotle, before turning to Neoplatonic and Stoic approaches to the soul and its care. It then considers the encounter between Greek and biblical traditions, Augustine’s accounts of love and moral psychology, and the historical and philosophical questions raised by Freud’s psychoanalysis.

Designed for students across disciplines, the course combines historical understanding with close reading and critical discussion. It connects the philosophy of emotions with ethics, philosophical psychology and the intellectual history of medicine.

This English guide presents the course for an international readership. The syllabus specifies bilingual instruction. It is a course information page, rather than an announcement of a current enrolment period.
02 / LEARNING OUTCOMES

What students will learn

On completing the course, students should be able to:

  1. Outline major developments in European philosophical accounts of emotions and their relationship to later psychological thought.
  2. Explain central questions and methods in the study of emotions and moral psychology.
  3. Read and interpret selected primary philosophical texts.
  4. Compare arguments across historical periods and intellectual traditions.
  5. Connect philosophical questions about emotions with their own disciplinary studies.
  6. Develop and communicate a reasoned position through discussion, oral presentation and written work.
03 / THEMATIC SYLLABUS

Four themes, eleven sessions

Theme I · Classical foundations

01

Plato: emotions and the structure of the soul

Early, middle and later accounts of emotions; the development of models of the soul; and the tensions within Plato’s philosophical psychology.

02

Aristotle: emotions, virtue and practical life

The structure of the soul, readings from the Nicomachean Ethics and Politics, and the practical significance of emotions.

03

Seminar I: Plato and Aristotle in dialogue

Close reading, student presentations and debate on continuity and difference between the two thinkers.

Theme II · The care of the soul

04

Neoplatonism: the soul and ideals of life

Plotinus’ account of the soul, the medical ideas discussed in relation to Neoplatonic psychology, and philosophical approaches to emotional life.

05

Stoicism: emotions and philosophical therapy

Major Stoic thinkers, accounts of the soul, the relationship between philosophy and medicine, and the strengths and limits of Stoic approaches to emotions.

06

Seminar II: philosophical therapy and medicine

Discussion of the connections and distinctions between caring for the soul and medical explanations of emotional life.

Theme III · Augustine and the medieval inheritance

07

Greek and biblical traditions in encounter

Historical contexts in which inherited accounts of emotions and their value were reconsidered and transformed.

08

Augustine: two cities and two loves

Augustine’s moral psychology, its classical sources, Neoplatonic and Stoic connections, and its influence on medieval thought.

09

Seminar III: love, evil and the transformation of emotions

The meaning of love, the origins of evil, and the relationship between emotional transformation and the two cities.

Theme IV · Modern transformations and psychoanalysis

10

Freud: desire, the unconscious and psychoanalysis

Freud’s intellectual context, accounts of the unconscious, the id, ego and superego, the interpretation of dreams, and the philosophical significance of psychoanalysis.

11

Seminar IV: philosophical traditions and psychoanalysis

Student presentations and discussion connecting the historical study of emotions with modern psychoanalytic questions, alongside submission of the final assignment.

04 / TEACHING & ASSESSMENT

Reading, dialogue and argument

Teaching combines lectures, guided primary-text reading, discussion, student presentations and debate. Students practise both introductory reading and detailed interpretation of selected passages, before developing their own arguments.

10% Class participation
50% Discussion and presentations
40% Final assignment

Assessment evaluates engagement with readings, understanding of concepts, the use of evidence and the clarity of argument. Task details and submission arrangements are provided for each course offering.
