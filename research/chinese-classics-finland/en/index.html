Chinese Philosophical Classics
in Finland

Early Translation, Introduction and Reception

Gao Yuan · Original Chinese article · 2021, No. 6, pp. 126–135

01 / RESEARCH OVERVIEW

Beyond the major European languages

How did Chinese philosophical classics enter the Finnish intellectual world? This study moves beyond accounts centred on the major languages of continental Europe and examines Finnish encounters with Daoist, Confucian and Buddhist texts.

Its principal focus is early transmission before the mid-twentieth century. It connects translation with national cultural awakening, academic institutions, travel, documentary collecting and missionary activity, while tracing selected later developments to clarify the longer trajectory of reception.

This page is an English research overview, not a published English translation of the article. Consult the original Chinese publication for quotations, documentary evidence and references.
02 / HISTORICAL FOUNDATIONS

Three routes towards knowledge of China

Academic institutions

The article examines the Royal Academy of Åbo and its intellectual legacy, including early accounts associated with Israel Reinius and Peter Johan Bladh. Academic institutions supplied an important framework for collecting knowledge about China. The subsequent relocation to Helsinki and the development of Finnish national culture form part of this background.

Exploration and collecting

Hugo Lund and C. G. E. Mannerheim helped expand the documentary and material resources through which China became known in Finland. Their journeys are examined as elements in a broader history of knowledge formation, rather than as isolated episodes preceding translation.

Missionary encounters

Finnish missionaries' reports, travel accounts and religious discussions supplied another route into Chinese thought. Their interest in local beliefs and ethical ideas helped turn the translation of classics into an early form of Sino-Finnish religious and philosophical exchange.

03 / DAOIST CLASSICS

The Daodejing: relay translation and interpretation

The article identifies the Daodejing, rather than a Confucian text, as the earliest Chinese philosophical classic translated into Finnish. Pekka Ervast's work appeared in excerpts in 1907 and was subsequently published in a systematic form in 1925.

Without knowledge of Chinese, Ervast worked through translations in other European languages. Gao examines these intermediary sources alongside Ervast's theosophical interests and Lutheran cultural background, analysing his use of God, the Logos and the Gospel of John to explain Dao.

Toivo Koskikallio's 1950 translation continued a comparative religious orientation. These cases show how translators constructed points of access through concepts familiar to Finnish readers.

Conceptual analogy is not equivalence. The article also considers the differences between Dao and Christian understandings of God, alongside the communicative value of their comparison.
04 / CONFUCIAN CLASSICS

The Great Learning: manuscripts and publication

Kalle Korhonen's 1921 Kungfutsen Suuri Oppi is a central case. Alongside translation, it presents Confucius, ethical teachings, religious questions and explanatory commentary.

The article also examines Koskikallio's earlier work on the Great Learning in 1907. Although the relevant manuscript was not then formally published, it influenced colleagues' interpretations. The history of transmission therefore extends beyond the record of printed books.

Gao considers the comparison of Confucian concepts with Finnish Protestant categories within the translators' social and religious circumstances. This approach explores both cultural asymmetry and the practical motivations behind contextual interpretation.

05 / BUDDHIST TRANSMISSION

Japanese mediation and Chinese Chan texts

Buddhist transmission followed a later and more indirect course. Yrjö Kallinen's 1944 work on Zen provides an important example of reception mediated by Japanese Zen and its European circulation, including the influence of D. T. Suzuki.

Later publications introduced Chinese and Japanese Zen stories, selections of Chinese thought, and texts such as the Heart Sutra and the Platform Sutra. Subsequent direct translation and interpretations informed by religious practice further expanded the Finnish understanding of Chinese Buddhism.

The study distinguishes these layers of mediation. Early Finnish Zen reception should not simply be treated as a comprehensive or direct encounter with Chinese Buddhist traditions.

06 / CONTRIBUTION & METHOD

Translation as situated intellectual exchange

A connected history of transmission

The study brings academic institutions, explorers and missionaries into a shared account. Translation emerges from networks of knowledge, collections and religious encounter, rather than from texts alone.

Explaining the prevalence of relay translation

Language skills, access to sources, cultural distance and wartime conditions help explain why indirect translations outnumbered direct ones. Relay translation had limitations, but also performed a practical role in early knowledge transfer.

Contextualisation and two-way communication

Finnish religious categories could help local readers understand Chinese thought; conversely, Chinese concepts could assist missionaries in explaining their own beliefs. The article treats this as a two-way process requiring attention to both context and conceptual differences.

Questions for digital humanities

The conclusion considers the continuing importance of translators' agency, textual reasoning and the interaction between original author, translator and reader in digital and AI-assisted environments. This is a methodological prospect, not a claim to have constructed a database or AI system.
