ABSTRACT · JOURNAL ARTICLE

Published English abstract

Translation and Circulation of Ming-Qing Classical Chinese Literature in Northern Europe: The Impact of the Philosophical Spirit on Strategy and Features of Translations in the Hermeneutics of Sinology

The translation of Ming-Qing literature played a significant role in the overseas introduction of Sinology in the last century. However, there is a dearth of systematic study in the Chinese language of the circulation of these materials in the Scandinavian academic world. A possible reason may be that the languages of the five Nordic countries, which were rooted in the Augustinian-Lutheran tradition, have a large gap with the Chinese context. In confronting this cultural gap, it is important to consider how the terminology of classical works was imported into indigenous Nordic texts so as to facilitate their reception by local readers. This article provides an in-depth examination of the introduction of the Ming-Qing classical literature to five countries, namely, Finland, Sweden, Denmark, Norway and Iceland, with a focus on translation status, development outlines, general features and circulation models. Through a systematic analysis of the text, it explores the interaction of the two traditions between the distinct philosophical values and beliefs, with a view to better understanding the spread of classical Chinese literature to the Nordic countries.

How does literature enter another world of thought?

The article relates the forms of translation to interpretive choices and cultural settings. It examines how Confucian, Buddhist and Daoist dimensions of Chinese fiction interact with the local intellectual and religious resources discussed in Nordic reception.

01

Where do translations begin?

The study follows the gathering of source materials, relay translation through European languages and selective anthologies, alongside movement towards direct translation from Chinese.

02

How is a literary world understood?

It asks how philosophical terms, emotions and narrative voices in works such as Dream of the Red Chamber and Jin Ping Mei are rendered for readers in different settings.

03

What does the translator contribute?

Beyond the opposition between domestication and foreignization, it explores the translator’s agency in sustaining a dialogue among author, translator and reader.
