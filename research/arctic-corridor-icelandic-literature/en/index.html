Chinese Literature in Iceland Translation, philosophical interpretation
and dialogue across the Arctic corridor

How do Chinese literary classics enter an Icelandic world of language, storytelling and belief? Gao Yuan examines Hjörleifur Sveinbjörnsson’s translations and the philosophical questions they open.

Gao Yuan · Social Science Research
2022 · No. 1 · pp. 203–212

01 / Research overview

Chinese classics beyond Europe’s major languages

This article examines the Icelandic reception of Ming-Qing literature through the work of Hjörleifur Sveinbjörnsson, connecting comparative literature with translation history, Chinese philosophy and Nordic religious thought.

European reception is often studied through English, French and German. Gao shifts attention to Iceland: a smaller linguistic setting whose Old Norse inheritance and Lutheran and liberal theological traditions provide a distinctive context for reading Chinese texts.

The central case is Sveinbjörnsson’s 2008 anthology, Apakóngur á Silkiveginum: Sýnisbók kínverskrar frásagnarlistar frá fyrri öldum, published by JPV in Reykjavík. Its title may be rendered as Monkey King on the Silk Road: An Anthology of Chinese Narrative Literature from Earlier Centuries. This is a selection of narratives and excerpts, not a complete Icelandic translation of every work it includes.

Making a story accessible is only part of cultural translation. The deeper question is how its philosophical and religious meanings survive the journey.

02 / Texts and sources

Reconstructing a literary encounter

The study focuses on seven Ming-Qing selections: Romance of the Three Kingdoms, Water Margin, Journey to the West, The Scholars, Dream of the Red Chamber, the story of Du Shiniang, and Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio. The anthology also includes two modern works outside this article’s main historical scope.

Gao traces three kinds of resources behind the translation: Chinese editions, translations into other European languages, and European and American scholarship. These overlapping sources make the anthology more complex than a simple case of relay translation.

For Dream of the Red Chamber, the discussion identifies the Chengyi text issued by People’s Literature Publishing House in 1957 and reprinted in 1972, alongside consultation of David Hawkes’s English translation and scholarship by C. T. Hsia and Jonathan Spence. The case illustrates how direct engagement with Chinese texts intersects with an international interpretive network.

03 / Translation and readership

Storytelling, selection and adaptation

The anthology introduces Chinese literary history and gives readers contextual guidance through introductions, summaries, selected episodes and illustrations. Gao analyses how such choices shape the reader’s encounter with unfamiliar narratives.

Narrative continuity

The translator foregrounds plots and dialogue, while reducing poetry and some narrative details. This supports readability, but can diminish the original works’ expressive range and philosophical density.

Images as a form of mediation

In the treatment of chapters seven to eleven of Water Margin, Zhang Guangyu’s illustrated Lin Chong provides a visual narrative accompanied by storytelling. The article examines this adaptation as a way of conveying the plot rather than translating the source sentence by sentence.

Domestication, foreignization and the reader

Gao interprets the translation through domestication, foreignization and the relationship among author, translator and reader. Familiar expressions and accessible dialogue make the texts approachable; the encounter with a different historical and cultural world remains essential. These are interpretive categories, rather than a numerical assessment of translation quality.

04 / Philosophical dialogue

Preserving the depth of the Three Teachings

Confucianism, Buddhism and Daoism are central to the article’s interpretation of Chinese literary imagination. Their presence in the novels cannot be adequately explained through plot alone.

Gao examines Sveinbjörnsson’s discussion of sanjiao, or the Three Teachings, and the difficulty of rendering jiao through Icelandic religious vocabulary. Categories associated with a Nordic Christian setting may not map neatly onto Chinese conceptions of teaching, belief, ethical cultivation and ultimate concern.

The article argues against reducing the classics’ religious and philosophical dimensions to familiar moral lessons. Its constructive proposal invokes the relationship between dao and qi—the Way and concrete forms or manifestations—to preserve Chinese philosophy’s internal framework and vitality while opening dialogue with Nordic Lutheran theology.

This is a proposed direction for intercultural interpretation, not a claim that Chinese and Christian concepts are identical. Nor does Gao present the proposal as a rigid retrospective standard for judging every earlier translator.

05 / Scholarly contributions

From a translation case to a comparative framework

A smaller-language perspective

Icelandic reception broadens the geographical and linguistic scope of Chinese-European literary studies.

Textual mediation

Edition history, scholarly resources, narrative selection and visual adaptation become connected objects of analysis.

Philosophical depth

The argument links reader-oriented translation to the interpretation of Confucian, Buddhist and Daoist thought.

The Polar Silk Road supplies a wider framework for Nordic-Chinese cultural interaction. The article treats literary and intellectual exchange as an important dimension of that discussion; it does not claim that the anthology physically travelled along an Arctic shipping route.
